1. Learning goals and roadmap

Regression is not merely the act of drawing a straight line. Its purpose is to describe how the conditional distribution of a response Y changes with features X. This guide supports two related goals:

  • Statistical explanation: estimate effects, standard errors, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests, primarily with statsmodels;
  • Predictive modeling: build preprocessing pipelines, regularize, cross-validate, and evaluate out of sample, primarily with scikit-learn.

By the end, you should be able to choose a model from the type and data-generating process of the response, interpret coefficients under different link functions, and detect leakage, collinearity, heteroskedasticity, overdispersion, and inappropriate evaluation metrics.

ResponseCommon modelPython implementationMeaning of exponentiated coefficient
Continuous, roughly symmetricGaussian / OLSsm.OLS, LinearRegressionDo not exponentiate; change in response units
Binary or successes out of trialsBinomial Logisticsm.GLM(...Binomial), LogisticRegressionOdds ratio
CountPoissonsm.GLM(...Poisson), PoissonRegressorRate ratio
Overdispersed countNegative Binomialsmf.negativebinomialRate ratio
Positive, right-skewed continuousGammasm.GLM(...Gamma), GammaRegressorMultiplicative change in the mean
Exact zeros plus positive skewTweedieTweedieRegressor, sm.GLMMultiplicative change in the mean

A sound order of operations is: define the question and unit of observation, split the data, explore the training data, establish a baseline, diagnose assumptions, and touch the test set only once for final evaluation.

3. Choose a model from the data-generating process

Every linear model can be described with three components:

  1. A random component specifying the distribution of Y;
  2. A linear predictor \eta = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_1 + \cdots + \beta_px_p;
  3. A link function g(\mu)=\eta, where \mu=E(Y\mid X).

OLS is the special case with a Gaussian distribution and identity link. A GLM allows the mean-variance relationship to change with the distribution. Do not choose a family from a histogram alone. Consider how observations arise: binary outcomes represent success or failure, counts represent event occurrence, and exposure represents unequal observation time or risk volume.

QuestionQuick checkPossible consequence
Is the response bounded?Binary, nonnegative, or strictly positive?OLS may produce impossible predictions
Does variance rise with the mean?Compare grouped means and variancesPoisson, NB, or Gamma variance may fit better
Are rows independent?Repeated customer, hospital, or date?Ordinary standard errors and random splits may fail
Is observation time unequal?Policy-years, run-hours, population?A count model needs an offset or exposure
Where will the model be deployed?New time, site, or IID sample?The split must reproduce deployment conditions

4. Statistical language: estimates, intervals, and predictions

A coefficient estimates an average conditional association under the model, holding other included variables fixed. It is not automatically a causal effect. Causal interpretation additionally requires a defensible treatment assignment or design, adequate control of confounding, correct temporal ordering, and explicit identification assumptions.

A standard error describes uncertainty in an estimate; a 95% confidence interval is a repeated-sampling construction. p < 0.05 means neither that an effect is large nor that predictions are useful. For prediction, prioritize out-of-sample error, probability calibration, and improvement over a simple baseline.

python helper-functions
def coefficient_table(result, exponentiate=False):
    """Collect statsmodels coefficients, intervals, and p-values."""
    ci = result.conf_int()
    table = pd.DataFrame({
        "estimate": result.params,
        "std_error": result.bse,
        "ci_low": ci.iloc[:, 0],
        "ci_high": ci.iloc[:, 1],
        "p_value": result.pvalues,
    })
    if exponentiate:
        for col in ["estimate", "ci_low", "ci_high"]:
            table[col] = np.exp(table[col])
    return table

def regression_metrics(y_true, y_pred):
    return pd.Series({
        "MAE": mean_absolute_error(y_true, y_pred),
        "RMSE": mean_squared_error(y_true, y_pred) ** 0.5,
        "R2": r2_score(y_true, y_pred),
    })

5. Simple linear regression: start with the equation

Simple linear regression is Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+\varepsilon_i. The intercept is the conditional mean at X=0; the slope is the average change in Y for a one-unit increase in X. If zero lies outside the observed range, the intercept may be only a mathematical anchor. Centering X can make it meaningful.

python simple-ols
n = 180
hours = rng.uniform(1, 12, n)
score = 52 + 3.4 * hours + rng.normal(0, 6, n)
simple = pd.DataFrame({"hours": hours, "score": score})

ols_simple = smf.ols("score ~ hours", data=simple).fit()
print(ols_simple.summary())
print(coefficient_table(ols_simple).round(3))

grid = pd.DataFrame({"hours": np.linspace(1, 12, 100)})
pred = ols_simple.get_prediction(grid).summary_frame(alpha=0.05)

mean_ci_lower and mean_ci_upper bound uncertainty in the conditional mean. obs_ci_lower and obs_ci_upper form a prediction interval for one new observation and are wider. They answer different questions and should not be interchanged.

python simple-ols-plot
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.scatter(simple["hours"], simple["score"], alpha=0.45, label="observations")
ax.plot(grid["hours"], pred["mean"], color="C1", label="fitted mean")
ax.fill_between(grid["hours"], pred["obs_ci_lower"], pred["obs_ci_upper"],
                color="C1", alpha=0.15, label="95% prediction interval")
ax.set(xlabel="Study hours", ylabel="Score")
ax.legend()
plt.show()

6. Multiple OLS: formulas, categories, and interactions

Each coefficient in multiple regression is a conditional association with other included variables held fixed. The formula interface creates the intercept and dummy variables automatically. C(region) explicitly treats region as categorical; training * experience adds both main effects and their interaction.

python multiple-ols
n = 600
employees = pd.DataFrame({
    "experience": rng.uniform(0, 20, n),
    "training": rng.integers(0, 2, n),
    "region": rng.choice(["West", "Central", "East"], n, p=[0.4, 0.35, 0.25]),
})
region_effect = employees["region"].map({"West": 0, "Central": -1.5, "East": 2.0})
employees["salary_k"] = (
    46 + 2.1 * employees["experience"] + 4.0 * employees["training"]
    + 0.35 * employees["experience"] * employees["training"]
    + region_effect + rng.normal(0, 6 + 0.25 * employees["experience"], n)
)

train, test = train_test_split(employees, test_size=0.25, random_state=RANDOM_STATE)
formula = "salary_k ~ experience + training + C(region) + training:experience"
ols = smf.ols(formula, data=train).fit()
print(coefficient_table(ols).round(3))
print(regression_metrics(test["salary_k"], ols.predict(test)).round(3))

With an interaction present, the training coefficient is the training contrast only at experience=0. At experience x, the contrast is \beta_{training}+x\beta_{interaction}. Centering experience at its mean often makes the main effect more interpretable.

7. OLS assumptions and diagnostic plots

The essential OLS requirements are a correctly specified conditional mean, independent errors, and—when using classical standard errors—constant conditional variance. Residual normality mainly affects small-sample inference; it is not the primary condition for unbiased coefficients. Diagnostics should be combined with domain knowledge rather than reduced to one test p-value.

python ols-diagnostics
fitted = ols.fittedvalues
resid = ols.resid
influence = ols.get_influence()
standardized = influence.resid_studentized_internal

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(11, 8))
sns.scatterplot(x=fitted, y=resid, alpha=0.5, ax=axes[0, 0])
axes[0, 0].axhline(0, color="black", lw=1)
axes[0, 0].set(title="Residuals vs fitted", xlabel="Fitted", ylabel="Residual")

sm.qqplot(resid, line="45", fit=True, ax=axes[0, 1])
axes[0, 1].set_title("Normal Q-Q")

sns.scatterplot(x=fitted, y=np.sqrt(np.abs(standardized)), alpha=0.5, ax=axes[1, 0])
axes[1, 0].set(title="Scale-location", xlabel="Fitted", ylabel="sqrt(|studentized residual|)")

cooks = influence.cooks_distance[0]
axes[1, 1].stem(np.arange(len(cooks)), cooks, markerfmt=",")
axes[1, 1].set(title="Cook's distance", xlabel="Row", ylabel="Distance")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Curvature in residuals versus fitted values suggests omitted nonlinear structure; a funnel suggests heteroskedasticity; large Cook's distances identify points that strongly influence coefficients. An influential point is not necessarily an error. Verify it and report sensitivity analyses instead of deleting it automatically.

8. Heteroskedasticity, collinearity, and robust uncertainty

Under heteroskedasticity, OLS coefficients may remain useful while classical standard errors are wrong. HC3 is a practical robust choice in small and moderate samples. When observations are correlated within a company or customer, use cluster-robust uncertainty; time series may require HAC or an explicit correlation model.

python robust-ols
ols_hc3 = smf.ols(formula, data=train).fit(cov_type="HC3")
print(coefficient_table(ols_hc3).round(3))

# With a company_id, clustered standard errors would be:
# clustered = smf.ols(formula, data=train).fit(
#     cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": train["company_id"]}
# )

Collinearity inflates standard errors and makes individual coefficients unstable, although it need not damage overall predictions. Inspect the design matrix, dependence structure, and variance inflation factors (VIFs), then consider combining variables, changing the question, or using Ridge. Do not mechanically remove variables from a fixed VIF cutoff.

python vif
from patsy import dmatrix
from statsmodels.stats.outliers_influence import variance_inflation_factor

X_design = dmatrix(
    "experience + training + C(region) + training:experience",
    data=train,
    return_type="dataframe"
)
vif = pd.Series(
    [variance_inflation_factor(X_design.to_numpy(), i) for i in range(X_design.shape[1])],
    index=X_design.columns,
    name="VIF"
)
print(vif.round(2))

9. The unifying GLM framework

A GLM does not directly assume Y=X\beta+\varepsilon. It assumes that a link-transformed conditional mean satisfies g(\mu)=X\beta. Logit and log links keep fitted values inside valid ranges automatically.

FamilyTypical variance relationshipCommon linkValid mean range
GaussianConstantidentityAny real number
Binomial\mu(1-\mu)logit0 to 1
Poisson\mulogNonnegative
Negative Binomial\mu+\alpha\mu^2logNonnegative
Gamma\mu^2 times dispersionlogStrictly positive
Tweedie, 1<p<2\mu^plogExact zero and positive values

Maximum likelihood chooses parameters that make the observed data comparatively plausible under the model. GLM deviance measures distance from a saturated model. For the same data and likelihood, lower AIC tends to balance fit and complexity, but AIC is not a substitute for out-of-sample validation.

10. Binomial Logistic regression

Logistic regression models the log-odds of event probability p: \log[p/(1-p)]=X\beta. exp(beta) is an odds ratio, not a probability difference or risk ratio. Because a probability change depends on the starting probability, also report predicted probabilities for representative profiles.

python logistic-model
cancer = load_breast_cancer(as_frame=True)
X = cancer.data[["mean radius", "mean texture", "mean smoothness"]]
# The sklearn target codes benign as 1; define malignant as the event here.
y = (cancer.target == 0).astype(int).rename("malignant")

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=RANDOM_STATE
)
X_train_sm = sm.add_constant(X_train, has_constant="add")
X_test_sm = sm.add_constant(X_test, has_constant="add")

logit = sm.GLM(y_train, X_train_sm, family=sm.families.Binomial()).fit()
print(coefficient_table(logit, exponentiate=True).round(3))

p_test = logit.predict(X_test_sm)
print({
    "ROC_AUC": round(roc_auc_score(y_test, p_test), 3),
    "log_loss": round(log_loss(y_test, p_test), 3),
})

ROC AUC evaluates ranking, while log loss heavily penalizes confident wrong probabilities. For imbalanced events, also examine the precision-recall curve. A decision threshold should not default blindly to 0.5. Select it from false-positive and false-negative costs on validation data, then report the final confusion matrix on untouched test data.

python logistic-threshold
threshold = 0.30  # Example only; select from business costs on validation data.
y_hat = (p_test >= threshold).astype(int)
tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(y_test, y_hat).ravel()
print(pd.Series({"TN": tn, "FP": fp, "FN": fn, "TP": tp}))

# Probabilities and uncertainty for representative observations
probability_intervals = logit.get_prediction(X_test_sm.iloc[:8]).summary_frame()
print(probability_intervals.round(3))

Complete separation can make unpenalized Logistic coefficients diverge. Combine defensibly sparse levels, gather more data, or use regularization or a Firth method. A huge unstable coefficient is not evidence of a huge precisely known effect.

11. Poisson regression: counts, rates, and exposure

A Poisson rate model uses \log(\mu)=X\beta+\log(exposure). log(exposure) is an offset whose coefficient is fixed at one: twice the observation time implies twice the expected event count, all else equal. Do not enter exposure as an ordinary predictor with a freely estimated coefficient.

python poisson-data
n = 1400
counts = pd.DataFrame({
    "driver_age": rng.uniform(18, 75, n),
    "vehicle_age": rng.uniform(0, 18, n),
    "region": rng.choice(["urban", "suburban", "rural"], n),
    "exposure": rng.uniform(0.15, 1.0, n),
})
eta = (
    -2.4 - 0.012 * (counts["driver_age"] - 45)
    + 0.045 * counts["vehicle_age"]
    + 0.35 * (counts["region"] == "urban")
    - 0.18 * (counts["region"] == "rural")
)
mu = counts["exposure"] * np.exp(eta)
counts["claims"] = rng.poisson(mu)
python poisson-model
poisson = smf.glm(
    "claims ~ driver_age + vehicle_age + C(region)",
    data=counts,
    family=sm.families.Poisson(),
    offset=np.log(counts["exposure"]),
).fit()

rate_ratios = coefficient_table(poisson, exponentiate=True)
print(rate_ratios.round(3))
print("Pearson dispersion:", round(np.sum(poisson.resid_pearson**2) / poisson.df_resid, 3))

If the rate ratio for vehicle_age is 1.046, each additional vehicle-year multiplies the claim rate by 1.046, or raises it by about 4.6%, conditional on other variables and exposure. The intercept is the baseline rate when all numeric predictors equal zero and categories are at their reference levels, so centering often improves its meaning.

12. Overdispersion and Negative Binomial regression

Poisson assumes conditional variance equals conditional mean. Unobserved heterogeneity, event clustering, and omitted variables often produce larger variance. Pearson dispersion well above one, residual patterns, and too many extreme counts suggest overdispersion, although the full model specification must be considered.

The following code simulates Negative Binomial data with variance \mu+\alpha\mu^2 and compares Poisson with an NB2 model that estimates alpha.

python negative-binomial
alpha_true = 0.8
size = 1 / alpha_true
prob = size / (size + mu)
counts["claims_nb"] = rng.negative_binomial(size, prob)

poisson_misspecified = smf.glm(
    "claims_nb ~ driver_age + vehicle_age + C(region)",
    data=counts,
    family=sm.families.Poisson(),
    offset=np.log(counts["exposure"]),
).fit()

nb = smf.negativebinomial(
    "claims_nb ~ driver_age + vehicle_age + C(region)",
    data=counts,
    exposure=counts["exposure"],
).fit(disp=False)

comparison = pd.DataFrame({
    "model": ["Poisson", "Negative Binomial"],
    "AIC": [poisson_misspecified.aic, nb.aic],
})
print(comparison.round(2))
print("Estimated alpha:", round(nb.params["alpha"], 3))
print(np.exp(nb.params.drop("alpha")).round(3))

Robust standard errors on a Poisson model can repair part of the inference problem, but they do not repair an incorrect predictive distribution. NB models the extra variance explicitly. If zeros are unusually frequent, first ask whether they arise from a distinct process. Hurdle and zero-inflated models need a domain mechanism; a large zero fraction alone is not enough.

13. Gamma regression for positive, right-skewed outcomes

A Gamma GLM is useful for strictly positive costs and durations whose variance grows roughly with the square of the mean. A log link guarantees positive fitted values and makes exp(beta) a multiplier of the conditional mean.

python gamma-model
n = 1000
severity = pd.DataFrame({
    "age": rng.uniform(20, 80, n),
    "complexity": rng.uniform(0, 10, n),
    "channel": rng.choice(["online", "agent"], n),
})
log_mean = (
    6.2 + 0.012 * (severity["age"] - 50)
    + 0.09 * severity["complexity"]
    + 0.18 * (severity["channel"] == "agent")
)
mean_cost = np.exp(log_mean)
shape = 4.0
severity["cost"] = rng.gamma(shape=shape, scale=mean_cost / shape)

gamma_glm = smf.glm(
    "cost ~ age + complexity + C(channel)",
    data=severity,
    family=sm.families.Gamma(link=sm.families.links.Log()),
).fit()
print(coefficient_table(gamma_glm, exponentiate=True).round(3))

Gamma cannot accept zeros or negative values. Do not add an arbitrary tiny constant merely to make the model run. Decide what zero means. If the response contains structural zeros and positive amounts, use a two-part model—probability of being positive followed by positive severity—or consider Tweedie.

15. Production pipelines with scikit-learn

The statsmodels formula interface is convenient for interpretation. The scikit-learn Pipeline and ColumnTransformer ensure that imputation, scaling, and one-hot encoding are fitted only on each training fold, preventing leakage. Every data-driven preprocessing operation belongs inside the pipeline.

python sklearn-pipeline
numeric = ["age", "income", "tenure"]
categorical = ["region", "plan"]

numeric_pipe = Pipeline([
    ("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
    ("scale", StandardScaler()),
])
categorical_pipe = Pipeline([
    ("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="most_frequent")),
    ("onehot", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore")),
])
preprocess = ColumnTransformer([
    ("numeric", numeric_pipe, numeric),
    ("categorical", categorical_pipe, categorical),
])

logistic_pipeline = Pipeline([
    ("preprocess", preprocess),
    ("model", LogisticRegression(C=1.0, max_iter=2000)),
])
# logistic_pipeline.fit(X_train, y_train)
# probabilities = logistic_pipeline.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]

Regularization strength is a hyperparameter. Smaller Logistic C means stronger regularization; larger alpha in PoissonRegressor or GammaRegressor means stronger regularization. Tune it inside the training data, never by repeated inspection of the test set.

python poisson-sklearn
# Model a rate and weight by exposure; this corresponds to an aggregated Poisson target.
features = counts[["driver_age", "vehicle_age", "region"]]
rate = counts["claims"] / counts["exposure"]
poisson_predictive = Pipeline([
    ("preprocess", ColumnTransformer([
        ("num", StandardScaler(), ["driver_age", "vehicle_age"]),
        ("cat", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), ["region"]),
    ])),
    ("model", PoissonRegressor(alpha=0.05, max_iter=2000)),
])
poisson_predictive.fit(features, rate, model__sample_weight=counts["exposure"])
predicted_count = poisson_predictive.predict(features) * counts["exposure"]

16. Splitting, cross-validation, and metrics

A random split is appropriate only for approximately independent, identically distributed observations. Validate forward in time for temporal data; use GroupKFold when customers contribute multiple rows; use StratifiedKFold to preserve an event rate. Whatever the method, it should mimic actual deployment.

TaskRecommended metricsDo not rely on alone
Continuous valueMAE, RMSE, out-of-sample R^2Training R^2
ProbabilityLog loss, Brier score, ROC/PR AUC, calibrationAccuracy
Poisson countMean Poisson deviance, MAE, grouped calibrationOrdinary R^2
Gamma positive valueMean Gamma deviance, MAE or relative errorCorrelation alone
Explanatory modelEffect size, interval, sensitivity analysisp-values alone
python cross-validation
diabetes = load_diabetes(as_frame=True)
X_diabetes = diabetes.data
y_diabetes = diabetes.target

linear_pipe = Pipeline([
    ("scale", StandardScaler()),
    ("model", LinearRegression()),
])
cv = KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=RANDOM_STATE)
scores = cross_validate(
    linear_pipe,
    X_diabetes,
    y_diabetes,
    cv=cv,
    scoring={"mae": "neg_mean_absolute_error", "r2": "r2"},
    return_train_score=False,
)
print(pd.DataFrame(scores).filter(regex="test_").agg(["mean", "std"]).round(3))

17. GLM diagnostics and calibration

GLMs offer several residual definitions. Deviance residuals help reveal distributional lack of fit, while Pearson residuals are useful for dispersion. Plot both against fitted values and important features. Also inspect leverage, Cook's distance, nonlinearity, and grouped calibration.

python glm-diagnostics
glm_frame = pd.DataFrame({
    "fitted": poisson.fittedvalues,
    "deviance_resid": poisson.resid_deviance,
    "pearson_resid": poisson.resid_pearson,
})

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))
sns.scatterplot(data=glm_frame, x="fitted", y="deviance_resid", alpha=0.4, ax=axes[0])
axes[0].axhline(0, color="black", lw=1)
sns.scatterplot(data=glm_frame, x="fitted", y="pearson_resid", alpha=0.4, ax=axes[1])
axes[1].axhline(0, color="black", lw=1)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

# Compare observed and predicted counts by predicted-risk decile.
calibration = counts.assign(predicted=poisson.fittedvalues)
calibration["bin"] = pd.qcut(calibration["predicted"], 10, duplicates="drop")
print(calibration.groupby("bin", observed=True)[["claims", "predicted"]].mean())

A residual plot with no obvious structure does not prove correctness; it only means that plot did not reveal a conspicuous counterexample. Also examine omitted-variable risks, data-collection changes, and performance in important subgroups.

18. A general method for interpreting coefficients

An interpretation should state the unit of change, conditioning variables, outcome scale, and uncertainty. These templates are more informative than reporting a sign alone:

  • OLS: for one additional year of experience, mean salary changes by \beta thousand dollars, holding other variables fixed;
  • Logistic: exp(beta)=1.25 multiplies the odds by 1.25; it does not add 25 percentage points to probability;
  • Poisson/NB: exp(beta)=0.90 multiplies the event rate by 0.90, an average reduction of about 10%;
  • Gamma/Tweedie with log link: exp(beta)=1.08 multiplies the conditional mean by 1.08, an average increase of about 8%;
  • Interaction: the effect of one variable depends on the other, so calculate joint effects at meaningful values.

19. Common mistakes and repairs

MistakeWhy it failsBetter practice
Scale or impute all data before cross-validationValidation information leaks into fittingPut every preprocessing step in a Pipeline
Use OLS for binary, count, or positive cost targetsPrediction range and variance are wrongMatch Binomial, Poisson/NB, or Gamma to the process
Read a Logistic odds ratio as probability changeProbability difference depends on the baselineReport representative probabilities and contrasts
Ignore count exposureUnequal observation time creates false differencesUse a log-exposure offset or weighted rate model
Choose zero-inflated because many values are zeroA low Poisson mean naturally produces many zerosEstablish a mechanism, then compare calibration and test deviance
Select variables, thresholds, or hyperparameters on test dataThe test score is no longer independentSelect with training/validation; test once at the end
Delete every “outlier” automaticallyReal and important operating cases may be lostVerify provenance and report include/exclude sensitivity
Describe significance as causalityRegression does not remove confounding automaticallyState design, timing, and identification assumptions

20. Reusable modeling templates

These minimal functions enforce a train/test boundary and return the model, predictions, and metrics. A production project should also validate schemas, log operations, version models, and evaluate subgroups.

def fit_ols_formula(train, test, formula, target):
    model = smf.ols(formula, data=train).fit(cov_type="HC3")
    prediction = model.predict(test)
    return {
        "model": model,
        "coefficients": coefficient_table(model),
        "prediction": prediction,
        "metrics": regression_metrics(test[target], prediction),
    }

def fit_poisson_formula(train, test, formula, target, exposure):
    model = smf.glm(
        formula,
        data=train,
        family=sm.families.Poisson(),
        offset=np.log(train[exposure]),
    ).fit()
    prediction = model.predict(test, offset=np.log(test[exposure]))
    metrics = pd.Series({
        "Poisson_deviance": mean_poisson_deviance(test[target], prediction),
        "MAE": mean_absolute_error(test[target], prediction),
    })
    return {"model": model, "prediction": prediction, "metrics": metrics}

21. Final checklist

Before releasing a model, confirm that:

  1. The unit of observation, target, prediction time, and deployment population are documented;
  2. Splitting occurred before every data-driven preprocessing operation;
  3. The family, link, and offset or exposure match the business process;
  4. Nonlinearity, interactions, heteroskedasticity, overdispersion, and dependence were assessed;
  5. Coefficients are interpreted on the correct scale with intervals, not p-values alone;
  6. Metrics fit the task and beat a simple baseline out of sample;
  7. Thresholds and hyperparameters were selected only in training and validation data;
  8. Important subgroups, temporal drift, invalid inputs, and calibration were evaluated;
  9. Random seeds, package versions, feature definitions, and the environment are reproducible;
  10. The report distinguishes association, prediction, and causal claims.
