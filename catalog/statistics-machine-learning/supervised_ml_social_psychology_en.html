1. Purpose, Scope, and Learning Goals

Supervised machine learning (ML) learns a mapping from measured predictors XX to an observed label or outcome YY. In sociology, examples include forecasting survey attrition, housing instability, or program uptake. In psychology, examples include predicting a future symptom score, treatment dropout, or relapse. The common thread is that the model learns from cases for which the outcome is already known and is evaluated on cases it did not use for fitting.

This guide emphasizes prediction with social and behavioral data. It does not treat a predictive model as evidence that changing a predictor will change the outcome.

After working through the guide, you should be able to:

  • translate a substantive question into a regression or classification task;
  • build leakage-resistant train/validation/test workflows;
  • fit and evaluate interpretable models in R and Python;
  • choose metrics that reflect prevalence, costs, and intended use;
  • distinguish prediction, explanation, measurement, and causal inference;
  • assess calibration, subgroup performance, uncertainty, and dataset shift;
  • document a model in a way that a sociologist or psychologist can audit.
Responsible-use boundary. The examples use simulated data and low-stakes research workflows. Do not use them to diagnose a person, deny services, determine custody, make employment or criminal-justice decisions, or replace qualified clinical judgment. High-impact use requires domain review, validation in the deployment population, privacy safeguards, appeal mechanisms, and ongoing monitoring.

2. From a Research Question to an ML Task

2.1 Prediction is not the same as explanation or causation

Suppose loneliness predicts later depressive symptoms. A model may make accurate predictions because loneliness is a proxy for social isolation, chronic illness, neighborhood conditions, or earlier symptoms. Prediction alone does not identify which mechanism is causal.

Goal Typical question Appropriate design/analysis Defensible conclusion
Description How common is severe distress? Representative sampling, weighted summaries Estimated prevalence in a defined population
Association Which variables co-vary with distress? Regression with uncertainty and design-aware SEs Conditional association
Prediction Who is likely to have high distress at follow-up? Held-out evaluation and calibration Expected predictive performance in a target setting
Causal inference Would an intervention reduce distress? Randomization or explicit causal identification Effect under stated assumptions

An ML model may support more than one goal, but the estimand, validation strategy, and language must match the primary goal.

2.2 Common tasks in sociology and psychology

Field Outcome Task Possible use Important risk
Sociology Follow-up survey completion Binary classification Allocate neutral reminder effort Unequal contact or surveillance
Sociology Months until stable employment Survival prediction Program planning Structural disadvantage encoded as individual risk
Sociology Household income next year Regression Population forecasting Skewness, top-coding, survey weights
Psychology Future symptom score Regression Research stratification Scale reliability and regression to the mean
Psychology Treatment dropout Binary classification Offer optional support Stigmatizing labels and self-fulfilling decisions
Psychology Diagnostic category Multiclass classification Research only unless clinically validated Label validity, spectrum bias, clinical harm

2.3 Define the prediction specification before modeling

Write one sentence with all six elements:

Among [population], use information available by [prediction time] to predict [outcome] over [horizon], for [decision or research use], evaluated primarily by [metric].

Example: “Among adults enrolled in a longitudinal community survey, use baseline information to predict completion of the six-month survey so that all participants can be offered appropriately timed reminders; evaluate discrimination with ROC AUC and probability accuracy with the Brier score.”

3. Data Design: Labels, Units, and Leakage

3.1 The row must represent the unit of prediction

If the prediction concerns a person, all measurements from that person must remain in the same split. Repeated diary observations, therapy sessions, students within schools, twins within families, and respondents within neighborhoods violate simple independence.

Use a grouped split for repeated people or clusters, a time split for future prediction, and a site-held-out split when transport to a new clinic or region is the real goal.

3.2 Label quality sets a ceiling

Psychological outcomes are often scale scores or diagnoses with measurement error. Sociological outcomes may come from self-report, administrative records, or a linkage process that misses particular groups. Check:

  • operational definition and time window;
  • scale reliability and measurement invariance;
  • whether the label is observed equally across subgroups;
  • missing-by-design and survey routing rules;
  • top/bottom coding, heaping, and changes in instruments;
  • whether the label is actually a proxy for an institutional decision.

3.3 Leakage: information that would not be available at prediction time

Common leakage examples include using a discharge code to predict hospitalization, including post-treatment questionnaire items in a baseline model, imputing before splitting, and allowing the same participant into both training and test data.

The safe order is:

  1. reserve the final test set;
  2. estimate imputation, scaling, feature selection, and tuning inside training data only;
  3. choose the model with validation or cross-validation;
  4. evaluate once on the untouched test set;
  5. if the test set guides another change, it has become validation data.

4. Models: What They Learn and When They Help

4.1 Linear and logistic regression

Linear regression predicts a continuous outcome as a weighted sum of features. Logistic regression predicts log-odds:

ŷ=β0+∑jβjxj\hat y = \beta_0 + \sum_j \beta_j x_j     and     P(Y=1∣X)={1+exp[−(β0+Xβ)]}−1P(Y=1\mid X)=\{1+\exp[-(\beta_0+X\beta)]\}^{-1}

These models are strong baselines: fast, inspectable, and often competitive with limited samples. Their apparent simplicity does not eliminate assumptions. Nonlinearity, interactions, multicollinearity, separation, clustering, and missing-data mechanisms still matter.

4.2 Regularization

When predictors are numerous or correlated, regularization discourages extreme coefficients.

  • Ridge (L2L_2) shrinks coefficients and usually retains all features.
  • Lasso (L1L_1) can set coefficients to zero, but selected variables may be unstable.
  • Elastic net combines both penalties.

Tune the penalty inside cross-validation. Standardize numerical predictors and do not interpret lasso selection as proof that omitted constructs are irrelevant.

4.3 Trees, forests, and boosting

A decision tree creates if/then partitions. It handles nonlinearities and interactions but a deep tree is unstable. Random forests average many decorrelated trees. Gradient boosting builds small trees sequentially to correct earlier errors.

Tree ensembles often improve tabular prediction, but “feature importance” is not a causal effect. Impurity-based importance can favor continuous or high-cardinality variables; held-out permutation importance is generally easier to defend.

4.4 Support-vector machines, k-nearest neighbors, and neural networks

Method Strength Limitation in social/behavioral research
SVM Flexible boundaries; effective in high dimensions Scaling and tuning are essential; probabilities need calibration
k-NN Intuitive local prediction Sensitive to scaling, irrelevant dimensions, and sparse regions
Neural network Learns complex representations Usually data-hungry; unstable with small samples; difficult to audit
Text model Uses interviews or open responses Privacy, dialect bias, construct validity, and context collapse

Start with a meaningful baseline. Complexity is justified only by reliable out-of-sample improvement or a clear operational need.

5. Evaluation That Matches the Use

5.1 Regression metrics

For continuous outcomes with errors ei=yi−ŷie_i=y_i-\hat y_i:

MAE=n−1∑|ei|MAE=n^{-1}\sum|e_i|    RMSE=n−1∑ei2RMSE=\sqrt{n^{-1}\sum e_i^2}    R2=1−∑ei2∑(yi−y‾)2R^2=1-\frac{\sum e_i^2}{\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2}
  • MAE is in the original scale and is relatively robust to large errors.
  • RMSE penalizes large errors more strongly.
  • Test-set R2R^2 can be negative; this means the model is worse than predicting the test mean.
  • Report error relative to a substantively meaningful difference or the scale’s measurement error.

5.2 Classification metrics

Metric Question answered Caveat
Sensitivity/recall Of true positive cases, how many were found? Does not describe false alerts
Specificity Of true negative cases, how many were rejected? Changes with threshold
Precision/PPV Of alerts, how many were positive? Strongly depends on prevalence
ROC AUC How often is a random positive ranked above a random negative? Can look good when useful precision is poor
PR AUC How well are rare positives retrieved? Baseline equals prevalence
Brier score How close are predicted probabilities to outcomes? Mixes calibration and resolution

A threshold is a decision rule, not an intrinsic model property. Choose it from the costs and capacity of the intended workflow, then report the full confusion matrix.

5.3 Calibration

A model that assigns probability 0.70 to 100 comparable cases should see about 70 events. Inspect calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope, and a binned or smooth calibration plot. AUC can remain unchanged while calibration deteriorates in a new population.

5.4 Uncertainty and cross-validation

Use repeated or nested cross-validation when tuning many choices. Report the distribution across folds or bootstrap confidence intervals, not only the best score. With small samples, performance estimates can be much noisier than coefficient p-values suggest.

6. R Case Study: Predicting Survey Follow-up Completion

This simulated sociology example predicts six-month survey completion. The imagined use is offering flexible, neutral reminders—not withholding services. All predictors are available at baseline.

6.1 Simulate and split the data

set.seed(20260811)
n <- 1200

survey <- data.frame(
  age = round(runif(n, 18, 80)),
  baseline_trust = pmin(pmax(rnorm(n, 5.5, 1.7), 0), 10),
  prior_wave = rbinom(n, 1, 0.62),
  moves_last_year = rpois(n, 0.45),
  contact_modes = sample(1:3, n, replace = TRUE,
                         prob = c(.28, .47, .25)),
  region = factor(sample(c("Urban", "Town", "Rural"), n, TRUE,
                         prob = c(.55, .25, .20)))
)

lp <- -1.1 + 0.018 * (survey$age - 45) +
  0.30 * (survey$baseline_trust - 5) +
  1.05 * survey$prior_wave - 0.45 * survey$moves_last_year +
  0.25 * (survey$contact_modes - 1) -
  0.25 * (survey$region == "Rural")
survey$completed <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(lp))

# Stratify so both outcome classes are represented in each split.
train_id <- unlist(lapply(split(seq_len(n), survey$completed), function(ii) {
  sample(ii, floor(0.75 * length(ii)))
}))
train <- survey[train_id, ]
test  <- survey[-train_id, ]

prop.table(table(train$completed))
prop.table(table(test$completed))

6.2 Fit an interpretable baseline

fit_logit <- glm(
  completed ~ age + baseline_trust + prior_wave + moves_last_year +
    contact_modes + region,
  data = train, family = binomial()
)

test$prob <- predict(fit_logit, newdata = test, type = "response")
test$pred <- as.integer(test$prob >= 0.50)

confusion <- table(predicted = test$pred, observed = test$completed)
confusion

tn <- confusion["0", "0"]; fn <- confusion["0", "1"]
fp <- confusion["1", "0"]; tp <- confusion["1", "1"]
c(
  sensitivity = tp / (tp + fn),
  specificity = tn / (tn + fp),
  precision   = tp / (tp + fp),
  accuracy    = (tp + tn) / sum(confusion),
  brier       = mean((test$prob - test$completed)^2)
)

6.3 Compute ROC AUC without an extra package

# Rank-sum identity; ties receive average ranks.
auc_rank <- function(y, p) {
  n1 <- sum(y == 1); n0 <- sum(y == 0)
  (sum(rank(p)[y == 1]) - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * n0)
}

auc_rank(test$completed, test$prob)

# Simple calibration table. Use larger bins when the test set is small.
test$bin <- cut(test$prob,
                breaks = unique(quantile(test$prob, 0:5 / 5)),
                include.lowest = TRUE)
aggregate(cbind(predicted = prob, observed = completed) ~ bin,
          data = test, FUN = mean)

6.4 Compare with a small tree

library(rpart)  # Recommended package distributed with R

fit_tree <- rpart(
  factor(completed) ~ age + baseline_trust + prior_wave +
    moves_last_year + contact_modes + region,
  data = train, method = "class",
  control = rpart.control(cp = 0.01, minbucket = 35)
)

tree_prob <- predict(fit_tree, newdata = test, type = "prob")[, "1"]
c(logistic_auc = auc_rank(test$completed, test$prob),
  tree_auc = auc_rank(test$completed, tree_prob),
  logistic_brier = mean((test$prob - test$completed)^2),
  tree_brier = mean((tree_prob - test$completed)^2))

printcp(fit_tree)
plot(fit_tree, uniform = TRUE, margin = .10)
text(fit_tree, use.n = TRUE, cex = .75)

Do not select the tree’s complexity parameter by test performance. In a real analysis, tune cp inside training folds, then compare the final selected model once on the test set.

6.5 Subgroup audit

group_metrics <- function(d) {
  y <- d$completed; p <- d$prob; pred <- as.integer(p >= .50)
  c(n = nrow(d), prevalence = mean(y),
    sensitivity = sum(pred == 1 & y == 1) / sum(y == 1),
    specificity = sum(pred == 0 & y == 0) / sum(y == 0),
    brier = mean((p - y)^2))
}

do.call(rbind, lapply(split(test, test$region), group_metrics))

Differences may reflect sample size, prevalence, measurement, model misspecification, or structural conditions. A subgroup table is a diagnostic—not a complete fairness analysis and not a command to force every metric to equality.

7. Python Case Study: Predicting a Future Symptom Score

This psychology example predicts a continuous follow-up distress score. It uses only NumPy and Pandas so the core example is portable. A ridge model is fitted with the closed-form solution.

7.1 Simulate data and create the design matrix

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(20260811)
n = 900

psych = pd.DataFrame({
    "baseline_distress": np.clip(rng.normal(12, 5, n), 0, 30),
    "sleep_hours": np.clip(rng.normal(7.0, 1.2, n), 3, 11),
    "social_support": np.clip(rng.normal(5.5, 1.8, n), 0, 10),
    "stress_events": rng.poisson(1.7, n),
    "therapy": rng.choice(["none", "individual", "group"], n,
                          p=[0.48, 0.32, 0.20])
})

noise = rng.normal(0, 3.4, n)
psych["followup_distress"] = np.clip(
    3.0 + 0.66 * psych["baseline_distress"]
    - 0.75 * (psych["sleep_hours"] - 7)
    - 0.55 * psych["social_support"]
    + 0.85 * psych["stress_events"]
    - 1.1 * (psych["therapy"] == "individual").astype(float)
    - 0.6 * (psych["therapy"] == "group").astype(float)
    + noise, 0, 30
)

# Split before estimating any preprocessing values.
test_index = rng.choice(psych.index, size=round(0.25 * n), replace=False)
train = psych.drop(index=test_index).copy()
test = psych.loc[test_index].copy()

features = ["baseline_distress", "sleep_hours", "social_support",
            "stress_events", "therapy"]
X_train_df = pd.get_dummies(train[features], drop_first=True, dtype=float)
X_test_df = pd.get_dummies(test[features], drop_first=True, dtype=float)
X_test_df = X_test_df.reindex(columns=X_train_df.columns, fill_value=0)

7.2 Standardize using training statistics and fit ridge regression

numeric = ["baseline_distress", "sleep_hours",
           "social_support", "stress_events"]
# Pandas 3.x rejects writing standardized floats into an integer column.
X_train_df[numeric] = X_train_df[numeric].astype(float)
X_test_df[numeric] = X_test_df[numeric].astype(float)
mu = X_train_df[numeric].mean()
sd = X_train_df[numeric].std(ddof=0).replace(0, 1)

X_train_df.loc[:, numeric] = (X_train_df[numeric] - mu) / sd
X_test_df.loc[:, numeric] = (X_test_df[numeric] - mu) / sd

X_train = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X_train_df)), X_train_df.to_numpy()])
X_test = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X_test_df)), X_test_df.to_numpy()])
y_train = train["followup_distress"].to_numpy()
y_test = test["followup_distress"].to_numpy()

alpha = 8.0  # Tune within the training set in a real analysis.
penalty = np.eye(X_train.shape[1])
penalty[0, 0] = 0.0  # Do not penalize the intercept.
beta = np.linalg.solve(X_train.T @ X_train + alpha * penalty,
                       X_train.T @ y_train)
pred = X_test @ beta

mae = np.mean(np.abs(y_test - pred))
rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((y_test - pred) ** 2))
r2 = 1 - np.sum((y_test - pred) ** 2) / np.sum((y_test - y_test.mean()) ** 2)
print({"MAE": round(mae, 3), "RMSE": round(rmse, 3), "R2": round(r2, 3)})

coef = pd.Series(beta[1:], index=X_train_df.columns).sort_values()
print(coef)

The coefficients for standardized numeric features describe change per training-set SD, conditional on other included features. They remain associational. Dummy-variable coefficients compare categories with the omitted reference group.

7.3 Bootstrap test performance

def regression_metrics(y, yhat):
    return np.array([
        np.mean(np.abs(y - yhat)),
        np.sqrt(np.mean((y - yhat) ** 2)),
        1 - np.sum((y - yhat) ** 2) / np.sum((y - y.mean()) ** 2)
    ])

boot = []
for _ in range(1000):
    ii = rng.integers(0, len(y_test), len(y_test))
    boot.append(regression_metrics(y_test[ii], pred[ii]))

boot = np.asarray(boot)
interval = pd.DataFrame({
    "estimate": regression_metrics(y_test, pred),
    "lower_95": np.quantile(boot, 0.025, axis=0),
    "upper_95": np.quantile(boot, 0.975, axis=0)
}, index=["MAE", "RMSE", "R2"])
print(interval.round(3))

The ordinary bootstrap treats test cases as independent. Use cluster bootstrap resampling when participants are nested in therapists, schools, households, or sites.

7.4 Standard scikit-learn pipeline (optional extension)

Install scikit-learn in the environment first. The pipeline keeps imputation, scaling, encoding, and model fitting inside each training fold.

from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, KFold
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder, StandardScaler

num_cols = ["baseline_distress", "sleep_hours", "social_support", "stress_events"]
cat_cols = ["therapy"]

numeric_pipe = Pipeline([
    ("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
    ("scale", StandardScaler())
])
categorical_pipe = Pipeline([
    ("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="most_frequent")),
    ("onehot", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"))
])
prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", numeric_pipe, num_cols),
    ("cat", categorical_pipe, cat_cols)
])
model = Pipeline([("prep", prep), ("ridge", Ridge())])

search = GridSearchCV(
    model, {"ridge__alpha": np.logspace(-3, 3, 13)},
    scoring="neg_mean_absolute_error",
    cv=KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=20260811)
)
search.fit(train[features], y_train)
final_prediction = search.predict(test[features])
print(search.best_params_)
print(regression_metrics(y_test, final_prediction))

8. Cross-validation for Clustered and Longitudinal Data

Random K-fold cross-validation answers “can the model predict another exchangeable row?” That is often the wrong question.

Deployment question Validation design
New observation from a known participant Time-ordered within-person evaluation
Entirely new participant Group folds by participant ID
New school, therapist, family, or neighborhood Group folds by cluster
Future cohort after a policy change Train on earlier time; test on later time
New clinic or country Leave-one-site-out or external validation

For nested cross-validation, the outer folds estimate generalization and the inner folds tune hyperparameters. Every preprocessing decision that learns from data belongs inside the inner loop.

9. Missing Data, Sampling, and Survey Design

Missingness is not merely a software inconvenience. “Prefer not to answer,” skipped sensitive questions, device failure, attrition, and planned questionnaire forms can carry different information.

  • Describe missingness by variable, wave, outcome, and relevant subgroup.
  • Fit imputation only on training folds and carry it forward to validation data.
  • Add missingness indicators only with a substantive rationale.
  • Compare complete-case and principled multiple-imputation analyses when inference is also a goal.
  • If estimating population performance from a complex survey, consider sampling weights, strata, and clusters.
  • Distinguish training weights that change the learned model from evaluation weights that define the target population.

An ML pipeline does not automatically make survey weights irrelevant. The correct approach depends on whether the target is finite-population description, prediction for sampled participants, or prediction for a policy-defined population.

10. Interpretation Without Overclaiming

10.1 Coefficients and odds ratios

Exponentiating a logistic coefficient produces a conditional odds ratio for a one-unit predictor change, given the model specification. It is not a risk ratio, causal effect, or necessarily a useful measure of predictive contribution.

10.2 Permutation importance

On untouched validation data, shuffle one feature and measure the loss in performance. A large drop means the fitted model relied on that feature in this dataset. Correlated predictors can share or mask importance, so repeat permutations and show uncertainty.

10.3 Partial dependence and individual explanations

Partial dependence averages model predictions over hypothetical feature values; it can extrapolate to implausible combinations. Individual explanation tools describe the fitted model locally, not the person’s psychological mechanism. Use plain-language sensitivity checks and representative cases alongside any SHAP-style visualization.

Do not remove a protected attribute and declare fairness. Neighborhood, language, income, disability-related measures, or institutional history can act as proxies. Sometimes a protected attribute is needed to measure disparate performance even when it is not used for prediction.

11. Fairness, Ethics, and Governance

11.1 A practical audit

  1. Define who benefits, who bears errors, and who can contest a result.
  2. Document consent, lawful purpose, retention, access, and linkage risk.
  3. Examine label validity and missingness across groups.
  4. Report group sample sizes, prevalence, calibration, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and uncertainty.
  5. Test plausible threshold policies rather than choosing one silently.
  6. Conduct external and temporal validation.
  7. Monitor drift, overrides, unexpected uses, and downstream outcomes.
  8. Establish a stop rule and a route to human review.

Fairness metrics can conflict when base rates differ. The correct response is not to pick the most favorable number; it is to connect metrics to harms, rights, and the institutional context.

11.2 Psychology-specific safeguards

  • A scale is not automatically a diagnosis.
  • Clinical utility requires more than statistical significance or AUC.
  • Consider spectrum bias, comorbidity, medication, treatment access, and referral pathways.
  • Validate translations and measurement invariance across language/cultural groups.
  • Avoid alarming probability displays without a support pathway.

11.3 Sociology-specific safeguards

  • Structural inequality can appear as individual “risk.”
  • Administrative labels often reflect enforcement and access, not only behavior.
  • Predictive parity does not repair an unjust allocation rule.
  • Community participation and qualitative evidence can expose harms invisible in a metric table.

  2. Choose thresholds of 0.30, 0.50, and 0.70. Explain who receives additional reminders and which errors increase.
